Image generation

A diffusion model starts from random noise and improves the whole image at once over a series of steps, guided by the prompt at every step. One real Stable Diffusion run shows how the prompt shapes the image, which areas of the image each prompt word influences, and how a new prompt changes a finished image.

The model denoises a grid, not pixels: an array of 64×64 cells with 4 numbers each, which a separate network, the autoencoder’s decoder, turns into a 512×512 image. After the first step the grid is still noise, but the preview already has a layout: sky at the top, a dark cliff at the bottom and the red blob of a lighthouse in the middle. The preview is blurred because from pure noise the model can predict only an averaged image, and that is why one step is not enough. After the fourth step the shapes are clear, and the last steps add detail such as the texture of the rocks and the foam.

The denoising loop and how the generator is trained

What happens to the prompt and the noise

  1. Text encoder. In Stable Diffusion 1.5 it is the CLIP model (OpenAI, 2021). Unlike in an LLM, the text is a separate input and the image is not a continuation of it. The CLIP tokeniser splits this prompt into 13 tokens, adds start and end markers and pads the result to 77 positions; it cuts a longer prompt off after 75 content tokens. The encoder turns each position into a vector of 768 numbers. These vectors are computed once and fed into every step.
    aredlighthouseonarockycliffaboveastormysea,photograph+ start, end and 62 padding positions
  2. The loop, 20 times. At the start the 64×64×4 grid is random noise. The grid has 64×64 cells with 4 numbers each, and each cell corresponds to a square of 8×8 pixels. It can also be described as 4 channels of 64×64 numbers. An RGB image has 3 channels, one per colour, but the grid’s channels do not stand for colours. Each channel is shown here as a grey square: the brighter the point, the larger the number. At every step the generator receives the grid, the noise level and the prompt vectors, and predicts the noise. In SD 1.5 the generator is a U-Net, described below. With guidance it does this twice per step, and the sampler combines the two predictions (section “Guidance and seed”). Subtracting all of the predicted noise and rescaling leaves a grid that decodes to the preview of the finished image, the bottom row in the figure above. The preview is only a visualisation: what goes into the next step is the grid with part of the noise removed by the sampler.
    → preview
  3. The end. After 20 steps the grid is denoised and the shape of the image is visible in it. The autoencoder’s decoder turns it into a 512×512 image.
    decoder →
  • Stable Diffusion 1.5 (2022) is an old model. It is the example here because it is small enough for the whole run to be computed on an ordinary CPU and shown step by step. Newer open models such as SD3 and FLUX have the same loop and differ in three places: the generator, the training target and the text encoder.
  • Generator: newer models use a transformer in place of the U-Net, an older convolutional network that processes the grid through small windows and uses attention in only some of its layers. Training target: they predict the direction in which the grid should change, not the noise (flow matching, described below). Text encoder: a larger language model (T5 in SD3 and FLUX.1) works alongside CLIP and its 77 positions, so the model accepts longer prompts, up to 512 tokens for FLUX.1 dev in the diffusers library. FLUX.2 (2025) replaces both encoders with a single Mistral model, also limited to 512 tokens. OpenAI has not published the architecture of GPT Image, nor Google that of Nano Banana Pro; OpenAI has said only that image generation in GPT-4o is autoregressive.

Which parts of the image the model ties each prompt word to

At every step the generator computes, for each patch of the grid, attention weights over the prompt vectors (cross-attention: the mechanism from “Attention”, with queries coming from the grid and keys and values from the prompt). Brighter areas have a higher attention weight for the given word: “lighthouse” and “red” land on the lighthouse, “cliff” on the rocks, “sea” on the water, “stormy” on the sky and the sea. This is the path by which the prompt affects what appears where.

The maps come from the U-Net layers that work on the grid reduced to 16×16, which is 256 patches (inside, the U-Net shrinks the grid and then enlarges it again), and are averaged over all steps. Each map is one column of the attention matrix: the rows are those 256 patches, the columns are the 77 prompt positions. Most of the weight, 88% on average, goes to the start marker, like the start token in “Attention” (an attention sink); each of the five words shown gets between 0.1 and 2%, and two to three times more in steps 1–4 than later. Each map has its own brightness scale, so brightness cannot be compared between the maps of different words. A map is a hint about where the model used a word, not proof of which pixel depends on it. SD3 and FLUX have no separate cross-attention: the prompt tokens sit in one sequence with the image tokens and ordinary attention connects them.

  • During training a model predicts a known target: in an LLM it is the next token, and here it is noise. The prediction error is used to adjust the weights (see “Training”). Noise at a random level is added to the grid of a real image, and the Stable Diffusion 1.5 generator learns to predict which noise was added. Each training example covers one noise level, and after hundreds of millions of examples the generator can denoise from any level, so at generation time it can be called step by step starting from pure noise.

The numbers and the image are those of Stable Diffusion 1.5 from the run above; the noisy grid is shown through the decoder. FLUX.1 from “How models generate images, video and audio” uses a grid of 128×128 cells with 16 numbers each for a 1024×1024 image. The autoencoder is trained separately, before the generator, and then frozen. Generating from scratch needs only the decoder, not the autoencoder’s encoder; the encoder comes back when a finished image is edited.

  • The newer SD3, FLUX and most video models use flow matching, a variant of the same training with noise. In training the noisy grid is a linear interpolation: (1 − t) × image grid + t × noise, for a random t between 0 and 1. The generator learns to predict the difference noise − image grid, which for every number in the grid is the direction from the image to the noise. At generation time the sampler (also called a solver in these models) moves the grid a small step the opposite way, towards the image, and calls the generator again; the diffusers library does this 28 times by default for FLUX.1 dev.
  • The training data consists of images paired with captions. Increasingly, another model writes those captions: 95% of the captions used to train DALL·E 3 (2023) were model-generated, because internet captions are short and leave out which object is where. Training usually starts on small images and their resolution is then raised in stages, for example from 256 to 1024 pixels. After that, as with an LLM, the model is tuned on selected examples and on human preferences (see “Training”).

Guidance and seed

  • In a diffusion model the guidance scale controls how strongly the prompt influences the image. In the classic version, classifier-free guidance (CFG), which Stable Diffusion 1.5 uses, the generator predicts the noise twice at every step, without the prompt and with it, and the sampler amplifies the difference between the two predictions.

Guidance: two predictions and their amplified difference

The sampler uses the noise given by the formula: prediction without the prompt + 7.5 × (prediction with the prompt − prediction without the prompt). Each picture is the preview computed from one of these noise predictions. The figure shows the first step: the prompt alone changes the preview only slightly, while the amplified difference already gives a clear outline of the lighthouse. At scale 1 the formula reduces to the prediction with the prompt.

A real first step of Stable Diffusion 1.5, prompt “a red lighthouse on a rocky cliff above a stormy sea, photograph”. The whole run is above, in the figure “From noise to an image in 20 steps”.

  • For the generator to be able to predict without a prompt, the caption is dropped from some of the training examples. The method’s authors got their best results when dropping the condition from 10–20% of examples (2022, an ImageNet 64×64 model, where the condition is a class label), and Stable Diffusion 1.5 was trained with captions dropped from 10% of examples. A higher guidance scale gives less varied results, and one that is too high ruins the colours, as in the pictures under “Same prompt, different settings” at the top. The effect resembles lowering the temperature (see “The next token”), but the mechanism is different and doubles the number of generator calls, so it almost doubles generation time. Models with distilled guidance, such as FLUX.1 dev, learn this effect without the second prediction. Scale values do not carry over between models: FLUX.1 dev defaults to 3.5 in diffusers.
  • The seed is the number the starting noise is drawn from. With the other settings unchanged, the seed decides the layout of the image. Changing the prompt or the guidance scale changes the layout too, because the prompt affects it from the first steps. An LLM samples at every token (see “The next token”), while the DDIM sampler shown here draws only once, at the start. The same prompt, seed and settings on the same model version usually give the same result; after a version change they no longer do, so save the finished files.

Editing and reference images

Same image, new prompt

New prompt: “…on a snowy cliff above a frozen sea at night”. Strength says what fraction of the denoising loop to run on the original: 0 means no change, and 1 means the whole loop from pure noise, which is generating from scratch.

Inpainting: a mask marks the area to edit

New prompt: “a white windmill on a rocky cliff above a stormy sea, photograph”.

Edits: Stable Diffusion 1.5, guidance 7.5. Strength picks the starting point in the 20-step loop. Noise at that point’s level is added to the original’s grid (noise accounts for about 33% of the grid’s variance at strength 0.3 and about 98% at 0.9), and then the remaining steps run: six at 0.3 and eighteen at 0.9. The request for a night scene has little effect even at strength 0.9, most likely because the image’s average brightness survived under the noise. Inpainting: a version of Stable Diffusion 1.5 trained with the mask as an extra input, 20 steps.

  • Editing a whole image (image-to-image) and inpainting also need the autoencoder’s encoder, which turns the original into a grid. In image-to-image, noise is added to the original image’s grid at the level selected by the strength setting, and the grid is then denoised with the new prompt. In inpainting the generator also receives the mask and the original’s grid with the masked area hidden, and creates new content inside the mask (in the example above, from pure noise) with the rest of the image as context. Cells outside the mask go through the loop and the decoder as well, so pixels outside the mask can change slightly, as the example above shows. In GPT Image the mask is only a hint, and OpenAI notes that the model does not always follow it exactly. When pixels outside the mask must stay untouched, paste the generated area into the original in your own code.
  • In FLUX.1 Kontext (2025) a reference image, for example a face or a product, goes through the same autoencoder as the generated image, and its tokens are placed next to the tokens of the generated image. It is context for one call, not training. Unintended changes accumulate over successive edits. The Kontext authors measured these changes by comparing the face with the original. Pass the original as the reference in every edit and compare the result with it, and don’t keep correcting only the previous version.

Resolution, errors and cost

  • The counterpart of the context window is the number of image tokens, and it follows from the size fixed before the start; an image can later be extended past its edge, but that is a separate generation (outpainting). Doubling the side means four times as many tokens and, for FLUX.1, an estimated 5.6 times as much computation per step: at 1024 pixels attention is about 13% of a step’s computation and grows 16 times, while the rest grows 4 times (see the calculator in “How models generate images, video and audio”). APIs have limits: the GPT Image models generate images with a side of up to 3840 pixels and at most 8.3 million pixels in total (as of November 2026).
  • Fitting a larger grid into GPU memory is not enough: a model generates well only at sizes close to those it was trained on. Stable Diffusion 1.5, trained on 512×512 images, often repeats objects and breaks their structure when asked for 1024×1024. A large image can be assembled from overlapping tiles that are denoised in the same steps and averaged where they overlap: no seams show, but each tile sees only the prompt and its neighbours, so the whole can be inconsistent, for example with a repeating landscape. Generate the image first at a size the model handles, then upscale it and run it through image-to-image in tiles at a low strength. This order is more reliable: the layout is already fixed, so the tiles repeat objects less often. Little noise limits changes to the content but does not rule them out.
  • Hands and the number of objects: the model learns what parts of photos look like, but nothing forces a generated hand to have five fingers. Newer models get this wrong less often.
  • Text in images was the weakest point for a long time: Stable Diffusion 1.5 got letters wrong, and the autoencoder distorted small text. Models from 2025–2026 such as GPT Image and Nano Banana Pro usually render short text correctly, but OpenAI still warns of problems with the exact placement and legibility of text. When text, a logo or a chart has to be exact to the character, add it in code after generation.
  • Generate images at the smallest size the model handles well, using several different seeds. Pick a layout, then upscale the chosen image. Using the same seed at a larger size produces different starting noise and therefore a different layout. Add the rejected attempts to the cost of every accepted image.
