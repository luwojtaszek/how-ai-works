Voice, speech and music

A speech or music model often works like an LLM: a codec turns sound into numbers, and the model appends them one after another. Other models denoise the whole recording at once, like an image model, and the way a model generates determines its typical errors and how long a recording it can generate coherently.

Why a neural codec, and how it stores sound

  • One second of CD-quality sound is 44,100 samples per channel, too many for a model to predict quickly one by one. A neural codec compresses it lossily, like MP3 or the Opus used in browser calls, but stores the result differently: it describes each short stretch of the recording, a frame, with a fixed number of numbers taken from learned codebooks. That comes to a few hundred numbers per second, and only about a hundred in codecs built for speech alone. A model can predict such numbers the way it predicts tokens: each one is a choice of one entry out of about a thousand in a codebook. (Opus is an audio codec, an IETF standard from 2012; it shares only its name with the Claude Opus model.)
  • The codec’s encoder cuts the recording into short frames and turns each into a vector of numbers. It then stores that vector as several approximations, like writing a number digit by digit: the first codebook gives a rough approximation, and each next one encodes what the previous ones missed (residual vector quantization, RVQ). A codebook works like the embedding table from “Vectors and matrices”, only in the other direction: the encoder looks for the nearest vector and stores its number, and the decoder adds up the vectors the numbers point to. Below, such a number is called a code; for the generator, a code is a token. A layer of codes is the codes from one codebook, one per frame. So every frame has one code from each layer, and the layers are not successive moments of the recording.

How many codes per second of sound

  • An example with DAC, the codec in the widget: one second of a recording at 44.1 kHz is 44,100 samples. The encoder makes one frame out of every 512 samples, about 86.1 frames per second, and stores each frame as 9 codes, numbers from 0 to 1023, so 10 bits each. That is about 775 codes and 7.75 kbit/s instead of 705.6 kbit/s for uncompressed mono (44,100 samples of 16 bits). Other codecs give other numbers: EnCodec at 24 kHz has 75 frames per second with 2–32 codes each, and Mimi from Moshi (2024) only 12.5 frames per second with 8 codes each, so that long conversations take fewer tokens.
  • Each next layer of codes describes finer detail of the sound, which follows from RVQ. The first layers alone already give usable sound, because in training the codec often has to reconstruct the sound from only the first few layers (quantizer dropout). That is why one layer in the widget already gives intelligible speech, and each further layer brings the sound closer to the original. Generators rely on this order: they predict the codes of the first layers first, then those of the later ones.
  • Compression loses some information: the decoder can add plausible-sounding detail, but it cannot faithfully reproduce detail the codes did not keep. EnCodec at 24 kHz covers frequencies up to 12 kHz at most, and typical codec artefacts are a metallic “s” and smeared drum hits. According to its authors, DAC (2023) reaches better quality than EnCodec at a third of the bitrate, both in automatic measurements and in listening tests with experts.
  • DAC and EnCodec codes are acoustic: they describe how the audio sounds, and the words are stored in it only indirectly, so a model struggles to keep the content on track over a longer stretch. That is why AudioLM (2022) added semantic tokens, 25 per second, taken from w2v-BERT, a model trained on speech alone, without transcripts. They carry content and rhythm but little information about the voice: in AudioLM, a continuation of a recording made from semantic tokens alone almost never kept the speaker’s voice. In Mimi, the first layer of codes is trained to carry content, and the other seven fill in how it sounds.

Three families of generators and their errors

  • Most of today’s speech generators belong to one of three families. A codec language model (neural codec language model) appends codes one after another, like an LLM. A flow matching model denoises the whole spectrogram at once, like an image model; a spectrogram is a picture of sound, described below, and flow matching is a variant of the diffusion covered in “Image generation”. A hybrid combines both: a language model sets the content, and a second network the sound.
  • The widget shows four models from the first two families and what each computes in one pass of the network: the codec models VALL-E (speech), MusicGen (music, described below) and Moshi (live conversation), and F5-TTS, which generates the whole spectrogram at once.

Schematic: 6 frames and 4 layers of codes instead of hundreds of frames and 4–8 layers, and for F5-TTS 4 spectrogram bands instead of 100. It shows the order described in the papers, not measured times.

  • Codec language models. VALL-E (2023) gets the text as phonemes, the units of pronunciation, and a 3-second voice recording as the start of the sequence, and then appends EnCodec codes the way an LLM appends tokens. It first generates the first layer of codes for the whole utterance, code by code, and a second model fills in the other seven of the eight layers, so finished speech exists only at the end. Newer models, such as Qwen3-TTS (2026), generate all layers frame by frame and use a codec that stores 12.5 frames per second. According to the authors, they return the first sound after about 0.1 s.
  • Codec models make errors similar to an LLM’s (see “The next token”): nothing ties the text rigidly to the sound, so words are sometimes skipped or repeated, and the model can get stuck generating the same frame over and over.
  • Flow matching on a spectrogram (Voicebox 2023, F5-TTS 2024). A mel spectrogram (on a scale matched to human hearing) shows the energy of the sound in 80–100 bands from low to high pitch, separately for every hundredth of a second. A transformer denoises the spectrogram of the whole utterance at once in 16–32 steps, like an image in diffusion, and a separate network, the vocoder, turns the spectrogram into a sound wave, just as an autoencoder’s decoder turns the grid into an image. The length has to be known before the start: F5-TTS computes it from the number of characters in the text, which fails on numbers and abbreviations. Words are lost less often, because the network sees the whole text and the whole utterance at once, but the first word is ready only together with the last.
  • Hybrids, common among open models from 2025–2026: a language model sets the content and rhythm, and a second network produces the sound. CosyVoice 2 (2024) generates 25 semantic tokens per second with the Qwen2.5-0.5B model, and a flow matching model turns them into a spectrogram in short chunks, so playback can start before the rest exists. The second network gets the semantic tokens as an extra input, just as an image model gets the prompt vectors. VibeVoice (2025) pushes compression further: its codec stores only 7.5 frames per second. It describes each frame directly with a vector of numbers instead of choosing codes from codebooks, so according to the authors up to 90 minutes of recording fit in the language model’s context.
  • Live conversation needs a model whose generator and decoder work in short chunks, from one frame to a fraction of a second: a streaming codec model such as Moshi, or a hybrid such as CosyVoice 2. If you prepare a recording ahead of time, choose the model by sound quality. Turn numbers, dates and abbreviations into words before synthesis, because each family gets them wrong in a different way.
  • A long text is split into segments, and at the joins intonation and pace can change abruptly. Split the text at paragraph boundaries, use the same model and voice, and pass the neighbouring text if the API allows it: ElevenLabs has request stitching for this, which passes in earlier requests (as of November 2026). Regenerate only the segments that stand out.

Cloning, voice conversion, dubbing and live conversation

  • Cloning from a sample does not change the model’s weights: the sample is part of the input, like an example in a prompt. In VALL-E the sequence starts with the phonemes of the sample’s transcript (the transcript is supplied together with the recording), the phonemes of the new text, and the codes of the sample. The model appends the codes of the new utterance and keeps the voice of the sample, just as an LLM keeps the style of the start of a text. It can do this because it was trained on recordings of thousands of speakers (60,000 hours).
  • In F5-TTS the sample’s transcript and the new text form one string of characters. The spectrogram of the sample is the known beginning, and the model generates the rest of the spectrogram.
  • For cloning from a sample (Instant Voice Cloning), ElevenLabs recommends 1–2 minutes of recording rather than VALL-E’s 3 s. The clone copies the style of the sample: a calm recording gives a voice that renders shouting or laughter poorly. A professional voice clone is made, according to ElevenLabs, by fine-tuning the model (see “Fine-tuning and LoRA”) on at least 30 minutes of recordings. It is more stable on long texts, but it also picks up the noise and the microphone’s sound from the recordings.
  • Cloning someone else’s voice requires the consent of the person it belongs to, and the provider’s safeguards do not replace that consent. According to ElevenLabs, its Voice Captcha verification confirms only that the person submitting the voice took part in the recording, not that the voice is theirs. A watermark, an inaudible signal added to the recording, is detected only by a detector that knows it, and not every generator adds one, so a missing watermark does not prove a recording is real. Store the record of consent together with the created voice, and make it possible to withdraw it.
  • Voice conversion keeps the content, pace and intonation of a recording and replaces the timbre: one technique described in the literature keeps the semantic tokens and generates the acoustic codes anew with a sample of the new voice. It is sometimes called “speech-to-speech”, but today that name usually means a live conversation model, described below.
  • Classic AI dubbing is a pipeline: transcription split by speaker, translation, synthesis in each speaker’s cloned voice, and a mix that keeps the background. Translation changes the number of syllables, so you have to choose between naturalness, duration and fidelity, and a perfect voice will not fix a bad transcript.
  • Live conversation can also be built as a pipeline: speech recognition, an LLM, synthesis. Only the transcript reaches the LLM, so the pipeline loses laughter and tone, and every stage adds latency: speech recognition waits for the end of the utterance, the LLM computes its first token, and synthesis generates the first sound. In OpenAI’s 2024 measurements, the pipeline of that time answered after 2.8 s on average with GPT-3.5 and 5.4 s with GPT-4, while GPT-4o, which takes in and returns audio itself, answered after 320 ms on average. Today (as of November 2026) a pipeline with streaming at every stage gets down to about 1 s according to providers and is still common in production, because it leaves a transcript and lets you swap models; measure the latency in your own setup.
  • Moshi (Kyutai, 2024) is one model instead of a pipeline. It listens and speaks at the same time: every 80 ms it takes in the codes of the sound from the other speaker’s microphone and generates a text token (an “inner monologue” ahead of the sound) and the codes of its own voice. In practice about 200 ms pass between sound at the input and the reply.
  • In OpenAI Realtime, audio takes up the same context window as text: one audio token per 100 ms of your speech and one per 50 ms of the model’s speech, which is 600 and 1,200 tokens per minute. The window depends on the model: gpt-realtime-2 has 128,000 tokens, and the older gpt-realtime 32,000 (as of November 2026). A minute in which each side speaks for 30 s is 300 + 600 = 900 tokens, so 32,000 is enough for at most about 35 minutes of speech without pauses, and less when the window also holds instructions and tool definitions. Once the window is full, the API by default drops the oldest messages. As in text chat, the model processes the whole conversation so far for every reply, so the cost of a turn grows with the length of the session. Prefix caching (see “Prompt caching”) and trimming old turns help.

Music, sound effects and length

  • MusicGen (Meta, 2023) is a codec language model: 50 frames per second with 4 codes each, with the layers shifted by one frame as in the widget above. This lets one pass predict one code from each layer, while the code of the next layer for a given frame still comes after the code of the previous layer. MusicGen was trained on 30-second excerpts, so a longer track is generated with the sliding window shown below. As a result, key, tempo and arrangement can gradually move away from those at the start of the track.

Each next window gets the last 12 s of the previous one as its prompt and appends 18 s. Already the second window, which starts at second 18, does not contain the chorus from second 10, so the model has no direct access to it; a similar melody may return by chance or through traces in the last 12 s, but nothing guarantees it. This is the same mechanism as extending a video in “Video generation” and cutting off the oldest history of a conversation with an LLM (see “The context window and agents”). Defaults from the MusicGen code: a 30 s window, with each next window starting 18 s after the previous one.

  • Stable Audio generates continuous vectors with diffusion instead of codes from a codebook, with the duration given as a condition. The 2024 research model produced up to 4 min 45 s at once, because it compresses sound heavily and was trained on whole tracks, and Stable Audio 3 (May 2026) produces up to 6 min 20 s in its larger versions. So a long track can be generated without stitching, but a long context does not guarantee a good track structure.
  • State the structure of the track explicitly instead of counting on the model to remember it: use tags in the lyrics, such as [Verse] and [Chorus] (this is how the Lyria documentation describes them), or a section plan in ElevenLabs Music, where a single section can be regenerated.
  • Product limits (as of November 2026): ElevenLabs Music from 3 s to 5 min according to the service description (the API documentation allows up to 10 min), ElevenLabs sound effects up to 30 s with a loop option that repeats the same sound, Suno v6 up to 8 min in one generation.
  • Closed products such as ElevenLabs, Suno and Udio do not publish their architecture, and Suno’s open Bark (2023) says nothing about how Suno v6 works. Results are non-deterministic, so save a good result as a file, and before using music in a product read the current licence and download terms.
